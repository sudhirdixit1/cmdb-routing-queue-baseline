
\section{The registered benchmark design}
\label{sec:design}

The benchmark is a registered one: which event logs enter, which recorded
attribute plays the predictor, which plays the forecast target and which the
baseline were fixed by written rules before any result on a new log existed.
This section says what was fixed and when, what the rules found, and what the
corpus does and does not have in common.

\subsection{What was fixed, and when}

The rules that turn an arbitrary event log into an instance of this problem
were committed before any result on a new log existed, in \protocolfile\
for the corpus and \pilotprotocolfile\ for the literature pilot; the
version history shows the registering commit adding the protocol and its
fetch script and no result file, and six amendments were declared afterwards, each with its cause and
the result it changed (Supplement~\ref{app:protocol}); their dates are in
the protocol file. \textbf{This
is not a timestamped third-party registration}: what is offered is a file in
a repository whose history is under the analyst's own control, so any claim that a rule was
fixed in advance is a claim about the repository and no stronger than the
repository. What the design buys is that the rules are legible, dated and
amendable only on the record, not that an outside party watched them being
written.

\subsection{Roles}

A log is admitted if the registered rules can find three things in it without
reading an outcome.

\begin{description}
\item[The register $f$.] A high-cardinality attribute that is not
resource-like, has at least \minCardF\ distinct values, is reused across at
least \minReuseF\ cases on average, and --- by amendment~1 --- whose values
recur across the temporal split, so that a document number is not mistaken for
a maintained entity.
\item[The free field $g$.] A resource-like attribute, present on at least
\minGPresent\ of cases, whose value varies within a case, so that it can carry
information about routing. Only the \emph{first} value it takes on a
case is used as a feature, while the handover target is defined from the
whole sequence of values --- so the feature is what is known when the case
opens and the target is what happens afterwards, and the field cannot inform
the model about its own future. The distinction is not cosmetic: the same
attribute supplies both, and using the case-level set would put the answer in
the features.
\item[The intake block $B_0$.] Every remaining attribute of cardinality at
most \maxCardBZero\ that is neither an outcome, nor a timestamp, nor a
relabelling of the activity, nor a deterministic coarsening of $f$.
\end{description}

The baseline \emph{rungs} are the nested ladder built from these roles: the
intercept-only model; half of the intake block (its lower-cardinality half,
fixed by a deterministic order before any outcome is seen); the whole intake
block; the block plus the free field $g$; and, where a log admits a further
field, that field as a rung of its own --- on the primary log the knowledge
reference, a pointer to a knowledge-base article. The intercept-only rung is
in the surface and excluded from every admissible set (Table~\ref{tab:axes});
Figure~\ref{fig:speccurve} labels the primary log's ladder.

Coarsenings of $f$ --- its type, its subtype, the service it belongs to ---
are given their own role rather than being swept into $B_0$, because which
\emph{layer} of a register pays is a separate question, answered in
Supplement~\ref{app:programmeread}.

\subsection{Targets, splits and exclusions}
\label{sec:targets}

Two targets are defined for every log --- \emph{handover}, whether more than
one resource group touches the case, and \emph{duration}, whether it runs
longer than the training half's median. Cases are ordered by first timestamp
and split $70/30$, with \nFolds\ expanding-window rolling-origin folds
beside it, and fields determined only at closure are excluded by name
\citep{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage,weytjens2022leakage}. A pair is
excluded only for a registered reason, and every admitted pair is
analysed and reported --- running the surface only where the reduction
resolves would condition the analysis on the outcome.
Supplement~\ref{app:targets} gives the rules and the exclusion codes, and
Supplement~\ref{app:margin} the admission margin at the upper edge of the
prevalence window.

Two exclusions a reader of Table~\ref{tab:roles} will ask about.
BPIC15\_2 is excluded under \textsc{small\_n} at \nBpicFifteenTwoRows\ cases
while its four siblings are admitted --- the rule is a floor on the case count
and nothing about the log's content --- and the further BPIC15 targets, the
BPIC20 logs and BPIC12 are excluded under \textsc{no\_headroom} or
\textsc{no\_g}, which are prevalence and role failures. And the
registered handover target on the case study's log sits at the upper edge of
the admission window: test prevalence \prevAdmittedMax\ against a
window that closes at \prevHi, which excludes other logs at
\prevExcludedAboveMin. That pair carries \nCellsBpicFourteenHandover\ of the
corpus's \nDeclaredAdmissibleScalar\ admissible cells --- the log carries
\nCellsBpicFourteen\ across its two targets --- so a corpus statistic pooled
over cells is partly a statement about it; Supplement~\ref{sec:family} reports
every headline on the IT service management (ITSM) family separately for this reason, and
Section~\ref{sec:lim} asks whether the window should be tightened.

\subsection{What the corpus does and does not have in common}
\label{sec:construct}

The corpus is \nLogs\ public logs spanning \nDomains\ domains, and it is
heterogeneous: this is a \emph{benchmark of specification sensitivity across
heterogeneous forecasting problems}, not a replication of one finding on
several organisations, so no effect magnitude is averaged across domains as
though it estimated one universal register value, and the \nITSMPairs\
ITSM-like pairs are analysed as a group as well. Supplement~\ref{app:construct}
names, for every pair, the domain, the field playing each role, the target's
interpretation and the construct threat that pair carries.
