## 1. What this is, in one paragraph

An evaluation-methodology paper for Elsevier's *Information Systems*: what a
recorded field is worth to a prediction is not one number but a **surface**
over a declared design space, and the paper supplies four objects for
reporting one (an exact functional-ANOVA decomposition, simultaneous max-$t$
bands from a pipeline-refitting bootstrap, *resolution regions* with a
robustness index, and *specification regret*), evidence on 19 log–target pairs
from 13 public event logs, and a case study on a bank's configuration
management database. Two documents are built: the **article**
(`paper/specification_surfaces.pdf`, 48 pp) and its **supplement**
(`paper/supplement.pdf`, 79 pp).

**The governing discipline:** every number in both documents is a LaTeX macro
in `paper/numbers.tex`, generated by `scripts/make_numbers.py` from
`results/*.csv`. **Never type a number into the prose.** A linter fails the
build if you do.

## 6. What is missing — the work, in priority order

### A. Validate the band's family-wise coverage — **DONE, round 25**

`scripts/s41_bandcoverage.py`. The band does not attain its nominal level
anywhere in this corpus's configuration, and the experiment is rigged in its
favour --- the bootstrap draws come from the sampling distribution itself, so
every coverage is an upper bound. Gaussian draws: median 87.3%, minimum
74.8%. The corpus's tails: 84.6% and 76.1%. Three things follow. The
safeguard the resolved flag uses --- the upper end of the critical value's
Monte Carlo interval --- moves coverage by less than a point. The binding
constraint is the draw count, not the estimator: 82.0% at 33 draws, 95.8% at
400, and the per-cell interval underneath is itself only 90.3% at 33. And a
Rademacher wild multiplier, the natural repair for a tail problem, is worse
than the Gaussian one.

**We did not switch to a wider estimator**, and section 11 says why: the one
that helps is the empirical quantile at the upper end of its order-statistic
interval, not the Monte Carlo upper end the paper already calls conservative,
and it reaches only 92.2% at 1.52 times the width. That is a judgement call
and it is the first thing to revisit.

The useful summary is the **shortfall factor** --- the multiplicative
widening that would have closed the gap, which is comparable with the
coverage calibration because both widen the same critical value. Surface
families need 1.05 to 1.24 and the calibration already applies 1.09 to 1.88.
Decision-curve families need 1.90 to 2.05 **and the calibration does not
touch them**. So the decision-curve conclusions are the ones to distrust.

Along the way: "these draws are heavy-tailed" is two different mechanisms
under one word, and on the decision-curve families it is largely **841
degenerate cells** whose net-benefit difference is zero by arithmetic.

### B. Widen the coverage calibration plane in *n* — **DONE, round 25**

The plane now runs 500 to 180,000 training rows over 33 cells with three
constant-ratio ladders, and all 19 pairs are inside it. `--resume` means a
plane that gains a cell costs a cell.

**$K/n$ is not sufficient and now it is measured**: log *n* enters at
t = 8.3, and t = 5.2 excluding every cell above 8,000 rows. The old plane
gave t = 1.45 for want of leverage. Adding log *n* to a parametric curve does
not repair the applied curve and makes it worse on the criterion the monotone
curve was chosen under, so the residual is reported.

**The estimator stops existing past a crossing in *n*.** The factor widens an
interval about its point estimate, and the basic interval does not always
contain its point estimate: the bootstrap displacement is a bias that does
not shrink with *n* while the half-width shrinks like n^-1/2. Four of 33
cells have no factor. **74 of the corpus's 3,900 whole-surface cells have a
band excluding their own point estimate**, 21% of UCI498/duration's. The
interval construction was chosen (section 10.2) on evidence from *one* sample
size, 4,000, below the regime where it misbehaves --- re-running that
comparison across the plane's range is the clearest next step.

No region label changed and the resolved count moved 892 to 896.

## 7. Rules of the road

1. **Never type a number into prose.** Add a macro in `round21_numbers.py`
   (or `round20_numbers.py`), regenerate, and use it.
2. **Every interval endpoint must come from the run the manuscript names.**
   `INTERVAL_SOURCES` in `round21_verify.py` enforces it — a contrast was once
   printed with two different intervals from two runs.
3. **`ESTIMANDS` in `round21_verify.py`** declares which macros may hold a
   quantity a reader would name in one phrase. Two members that disagree is a
   build failure. Add an entry whenever you create a second macro for one idea.
4. **`to_latex(escape=True)` escapes what you pre-escape.** Write `50%`, not
   `50\%`; never put math in a column heading.
5. **A `p{}` column narrower than its longest unbreakable token does not wrap
   — it spills**, and `\resizebox` cannot see it because the cell still
   measures its declared width. Check `textcols` widths against content.
6. **`build_journal.py` reports both documents' overfull counts.** Both must
   be zero. It used to report only the article's, and 70 boxes in the
   supplement went unnoticed for four rounds.
7. **A macro is only as safe as its definition.** The false coverage sentence
   came from a macro that was an extremum over a *selected subset* and matched
   its source exactly, so the verifier passed. When you add a macro, write
   down what set it ranges over.
8. Bash heredocs here mangle backslashes — use the Write/Edit tools for
   anything containing LaTeX, and beware `\r`/`\n` inside Python strings.

between them they checked roughly 100 printed numbers against the result files
and found no arithmetic error. The core findings have survived every round.

But three of three blind referees said **major revision**, and the most

against the referees' 32–40, reached by moving material to the supplement and
deleting nothing. The article is 48 pages once the journal's required
