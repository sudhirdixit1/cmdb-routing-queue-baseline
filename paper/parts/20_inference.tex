
\section{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:inference}

\subsection{The estimator}

The pipeline is frozen and identical on every log. Attributes are assigned to
roles by the pre-registered rules of Section~\ref{sec:design}; cases are
ordered by their first timestamp; and the split is temporal.

The learner axis spans encodings as well as model families: one-hot logistic
regression, frequency-encoded logistic regression, cross-fitted
target-encoded \citep{micci2001preprocessing} histogram gradient boosting, and
the same boosting wrapped in isotonic calibration. All four run on the case
study's log; the other pairs carry the first and the third, which are the two
that differ most in family, because the full grid on twenty-two pairs is a
compute budget this study does not have and a partial grid stated is better
than a full grid claimed.

The registers here run to \cardF\ levels on the case study's log, above the
cardinality cap of the boosting implementation's native categorical support,
which is why the tree learner is target-encoded rather than one-hot; the
encoder is cross-fitted \citep{pedregosa2011scikit} inside the training half,
so no training row sees its own outcome in its own encoding.

\subsection{Uncertainty that includes the model}
\label{sec:boot}

The interval that accompanied an earlier version of this work resampled test
rows with the fitted models held fixed. Such an interval excludes
training-sample variability, model-selection variability, encoder variability,
split uncertainty and the temporal dependence between cases, and a design
sweep is not a substitute for it: specification sensitivity and sampling
uncertainty answer different questions.

Every interval in this paper is instead a \emph{nested moving-block
bootstrap}. One draw resamples the training half by moving blocks
\citep{kunsch1989jackknife} of length $\lceil n^{1/3} \rceil$, refits every arm of every rung under every learner
and every quality mechanism on that resample, and evaluates on a moving-block
resample of the test half. Encoders, frequency tables, target encodings,
register frequency rankings and calibrators are rebuilt inside the draw. On
the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:sim}, where the truth is known by
enumeration, the fixed-model interval covers its own estimand
\coverageNaiveMedian\ of the time and the nested interval
\coverageNestedMedian.

Every log is also reported under rolling-origin validation --- \nFolds\
expanding-window folds --- beside the single temporal holdout, and the split
is an axis of the decomposition rather than a robustness paragraph.

\paragraph{Refitting inside the draw breaks the percentile interval, and here
is the fix.}
This is the one place where the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:sim} caught the
method rather than confirming it, so it is stated before the results rather
than after.

A bootstrap resample of $n$ rows contains about $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63\%$ of
the distinct levels of a high-cardinality categorical. The register on the
case study's log has \cardF\ levels, so \emph{every} refit inside \emph{every}
draw sees a sparser register than the real training half does, and the
increment it produces is systematically smaller. The bootstrap distribution
therefore sits \emph{below} the estimate --- in \shiftNegativePct\ of this
paper's cells, by a median \shiftMedian\ AUC --- and a percentile interval
taken from it sits below the estimand and misses no matter how wide it is.
That is not a small effect: in the sparse and noisy worlds of
Section~\ref{sec:sim} the percentile interval covers far below nominal, and
the nested one covers \emph{worse} than the fixed-model one, which is the
opposite of what refitting is for.

The fix is standard and we should have used it from the start. Every interval
in this paper is the \emph{basic} (pivotal) interval
\[
\bigl[\,2\hat{V} - q_{1-\alpha/2},\;\; 2\hat{V} - q_{\alpha/2}\,\bigr],
\]
which pivots the shift out, with the bias-corrected percentile reported beside
it in \texttt{results/s17\_cells.csv}. Simultaneous bands are recentred by the
same shift, which is a property of the draws and so corrects every cell of a
family at once. Table~\ref{tab:sim} reports the coverage of all three
constructions.

\subsection{Simultaneous bands}
\label{sec:simbands}

A decision curve read across \nGrid\ thresholds, or a ladder read across
rungs, is a family of statements, and a pointwise interval does not control
the probability that \emph{some} member of the family is wrong. For a declared
family $\mathcal{F}$ of cells with bootstrap draws $V^{(b)}_c$ and per-cell
standard errors $\hat\sigma_c$, let
\[
T^{(b)} \;=\; \max_{c \in \mathcal{F}}
\frac{\bigl|V^{(b)}_c - \bar{V}_c\bigr|}{\hat\sigma_c},
\qquad
q_{1-\alpha} = \text{the } (1-\alpha)\text{ quantile of } \{T^{(b)}\},
\]
and report $\hat{V}_c \pm q_{1-\alpha}\hat\sigma_c$. This is the max-$t$
(Romano--Wolf) construction \citep{romano2005stepdown} and has family-wise coverage over $\mathcal{F}$
asymptotically. Two families are declared: the scalar instruments at a cell,
and the whole decision curve at a cell. Across this paper's surfaces the
median $q_{0.95}$ is \maxTMedian\ against the pointwise $1.96$, so a
simultaneous statement costs a factor of about \maxTMedianRatio\ in width, and
the bands in Section~\ref{sec:dca} are wider than the pointwise intervals an
earlier version printed.

\subsection{Confirmatory and exploratory}

Five contrasts on the primary log are \emph{confirmatory} and were fixed in
\texttt{scripts/s02\_boot.py} before the bootstrap ran: that the register adds
to the intake baseline; that it still adds once the free field is admitted;
that it adds at the later decision time; that the absorption $D$ is positive;
and that a half-empty register is worth less than a full one. They carry
one-sided bootstrap $p$-values with a Holm step-down correction
\citep{holm1979simple} at $\alpha = 0.05$, and \nConfirmReject\ of the five
survive
(Table~\ref{tab:confirm}). Every other comparison in this paper is
\emph{exploratory}, is labelled as such where it appears, and carries no
multiplicity correction, because none would be meaningful over a surface that
is deliberately enumerated in full: the surface is not a search for a
significant cell but a description of all of them.

\subsection{Fieller intervals for the reduction}
\label{sec:fieller}

For $\vartheta = V_1 / V_0$ with estimates $(\hat v_0, \hat v_1)$ and
bootstrap covariance $(\hat s_{00}, \hat s_{11}, \hat s_{01})$, Fieller's
theorem \citep{fieller1954} gives the confidence set
\[
\Bigl\{\vartheta : (\hat v_1 - \vartheta \hat v_0)^2 \leq
z^2\bigl(\hat s_{11} - 2\vartheta \hat s_{01} +
\vartheta^2 \hat s_{00}\bigr)\Bigr\},
\]
whose boundary solves a quadratic in $\vartheta$. When the coefficient of
$\vartheta^2$ is positive the set is a bounded interval; when it is not, the
set is unbounded or is the \emph{complement} of an interval, and no bounded
interval is a correct summary. Of the \nFieller\ reductions this paper
computes, \nFiellerUnbounded\ have a non-bounded Fieller set. A percentile
interval reports a bounded interval for every one of them.

\subsection{The sensitivity decomposition}
\label{sec:sobol}

Because the surface is a factorial over its axes, the variance of $V_s$ across
specifications admits an exact functional-ANOVA decomposition
\citep{sobol2001global,saltelli2010variance}. For axis $i$,
\[
S_i \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Var}_{X_i}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[V \mid X_i]\bigr)}
{\operatorname{Var}(V)},
\qquad
S_{T_i} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\operatorname{Var}_{X_{\sim i}}\!
\bigl(\mathbb{E}[V \mid X_{\sim i}]\bigr)}{\operatorname{Var}(V)} ,
\]
the first-order and total sensitivity indices. $S_i$ is the share of the
disagreement between analysts explained by their choice on axis $i$ alone;
$S_{T_i} - S_i$ is the part that lives in interactions with the other axes.
Indices are computed twice: within each metric, in that metric's own units, so
no scale is mixed; and on the \emph{headroom} scale $V_s / (1 - m(B))$, the
share of the remaining distance to a perfect score that the field captures,
which is comparable across the scalar instruments and lets the metric itself
enter as an axis.

Twice, rather than once, because of the following.

\begin{proposition}[The decomposition inherits the metric's scale]
\label{prop:scale}
Let $\phi$ be strictly increasing with $\phi(0) = 0$. The first-order
sensitivity indices of $V$ and of $\phi(V)$ over the same design need not
agree, and there exist designs and $\phi$ under which one axis has the larger
index on one scale and a different axis on the other.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof (construction)]
Two levels per axis will not do, and the reason is worth stating: with two
levels the two main effects are $|P+Q|$ and $|P-Q|$, where $P$ and $Q$ are the
two within-axis differences, so their ordering is decided by
$\operatorname{sign}(PQ)$, and a strictly increasing $\phi$ preserves the sign
of each difference. At two levels the ordering is therefore \emph{invariant}
and the proposition is false. From three levels a conditional mean averages
more than two cells and a convex $\phi$ reweights them unequally. The
$3 \times 3$ design in \texttt{results/s07\_prop3.csv} has
$S_A = \pThreeRawA$ against $S_B = \pThreeRawB$ in its own units and
$S_A = \pThreeSqrtA$ against $S_B = \pThreeSqrtB$ under $\phi = \sqrt{\cdot}$,
so the leader changes.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is the same phenomenon as Proposition~\ref{prop:nonid}, one level up:
just as the reduction is not identified without the metric, ``which choice
matters most'' is not identified without the \emph{scale on which the
increment is expressed}. The manuscript therefore never says an axis dominates
without naming the scale, and Section~\ref{sec:multilog} reports both.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Resolution regions and the robustness index}
\label{sec:regions}

\begin{definition}[Cell labels and surface regions]
Under the simultaneous band of Section~\ref{sec:simbands}, a cell is
\emph{beneficial} if its lower band is above zero, \emph{harmful} if its upper
band is below zero, and \emph{unresolved} otherwise. A surface is
\emph{uniformly beneficial} if every admissible cell is beneficial;
\emph{conditionally beneficial} if some are and none is harmful;
\emph{sign-changing} if there is at least one of each; and \emph{unresolved}
if none is resolved. The \emph{robustness index} is
\[
\rho \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{beneficial}\} - \#\{\text{harmful}\}}
{\#\{\text{admissible}\}} \;\in\; [-1, 1].
\]
\end{definition}

$\rho = 1$ is the only state in which one positive number is a safe summary of
a surface, and the empirical question this paper answers is how often it
obtains.

The region is defined over the \emph{scalar} family. Folding the
decision-curve cells into it would let a single extreme threshold --- where
net benefit is near zero for every model and nothing resolves --- decide the
label of a whole surface. The decision curve gets its own region, reported
beside the first, and Section~\ref{sec:dca} reads that one.

The intercept-only rung is excluded from every admissible set. It is a
legitimate point of the surface and an illegitimate comparator: no analyst
builds a model with no features, and a reduction measured against one is
inflated by construction. An earlier version of this work reported a headline
that included it; Section~\ref{sec:multilog} reports what the headline becomes
without it.

\subsection{Specification regret}
\label{sec:regretdef}

A reader of a one-number claim decides whether to adopt the field, and the
decision rule the number supports is \emph{adopt iff $V_s > 0$}. If the
specification the reader will actually run is drawn from the same admissible
set, the \emph{misreport rate} of a report at $s$ is
\[
M(s) \;=\; \Pr_{s'}\bigl[\operatorname{sign} V_{s'} \neq
\operatorname{sign} V_{s}\bigr] ,
\]
and the \emph{regret}
\[
G(s) \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{s'}\bigl[\max(0, -V_{s'})\bigr]\,\mathbf{1}\{V_s > 0\}
\;+\;
\mathbb{E}_{s'}\bigl[\max(0, V_{s'})\bigr]\,\mathbf{1}\{V_s \leq 0\}
\]
is the performance destroyed by adopting when the reader's cell is harmful
plus the performance forgone by declining when it is beneficial.

Saying that a surface report asserts a sign only where the region is uniform,
and therefore misreports nothing, is a definitional win and not a measured
one. So the paper compares explicit \emph{decision rules} under the single
loss above, and one of them is optimal.

\begin{proposition}[The decision-optimal collapse]
\label{prop:optimal}
Let the reader's specification be drawn from the admissible set $\mathcal{S}$
under a weighting $w$, and let a rule choose one action for all readers.
Writing $A = \mathbb{E}_w[\max(0,-V_s)]$ for the harm mass and
$B = \mathbb{E}_w[\max(0,V_s)]$ for the benefit mass, expected loss is $A$
under \textsc{adopt} and $B$ under \textsc{decline}, and
\[
B - A \;=\; \mathbb{E}_w\!\left[\max(0,V_s) - \max(0,-V_s)\right]
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_w[V_s].
\]
Hence expected loss is minimised by adopting exactly when
$\mathbb{E}_w[V_s] > 0$: the decision-optimal summary of a specification
surface is the \emph{weighted mean increment}, not the sign at any one cell
and not the count of positive cells.
\end{proposition}

Three things follow, and they matter more than the proposition's difficulty.

First, the conservative rule --- adopt only where the whole surface is
positive --- is not merely cautious, it is \emph{wrong} whenever the harm mass
is smaller than the benefit mass it forgoes, which is most of this corpus.
Section~\ref{sec:rules} measures the cost.

Second, a one-number report is optimal exactly when the sign of its cell
agrees with the sign of the mean, which is a coincidence and not a property of
the cell.

Third, and this is the resolution of an apparent tension in the paper: there
\emph{is} a defensible collapse of a surface to one number, and it is
$\mathbb{E}_w[V_s]$. But it depends on $w$ --- on which specifications the
reader's own practice makes likely --- and the analyst does not know the
reader's $w$. So the analyst reports the surface and the reader collapses it.
\textbf{Collapse for a decision; report the surface.}

The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:rules} is run under two weightings ---
uniform, and concentrated near the conventional specification --- so that the
dependence on $w$ is visible rather than assumed away.

\subsection{Simulation against a known answer}
\label{sec:sim}

An earlier version's simulation fitted a logistic data-generating process with
logistic regression --- an unusually favourable arrangement --- on forty
replicates, which cannot resolve a coverage estimate, and dismissed an $85\%$
coverage as boundary behaviour. This version replaces it with \nWorlds\ worlds
and \nSimReps\ replicates each. The covariate space is finite, so the joint
distribution of cell and outcome is enumerated and the population AUC of any
scoring rule is computed in closed form rather than simulated. The worlds are:
a logistic surface, on which the estimator is correctly specified; a nonlinear
surface with an interaction and a threshold the logistic model cannot see; a
drifting surface whose coefficients move between the training and test eras; a
sparse register with \nSimSparse\ levels on the same sample size; an
imbalanced outcome at prevalence \simPrevalence; and a noisy register in which
a share of values are wrong.

Two estimands are separated, because under misspecification they are not the
same. $V_{\mathrm{oracle}}$ is the increment between the two Bayes-optimal
scoring rules --- what the register is worth in the world.
$V_{\mathrm{limit}}$ is the increment between the two fitted pipelines'
limits --- what the estimator estimates. A resampling interval can only cover
the second, and reporting its coverage of the first as though it were the
estimator's coverage is precisely the error the earlier simulation made. Both
are reported in Table~\ref{tab:sim}.
